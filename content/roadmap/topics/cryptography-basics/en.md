      - q: "Why does a website store passwords with Argon2id or bcrypt instead of SHA-256?"
        a: "SHA-256 is built to be fast, so someone who steals the hashes can try enormous numbers of guesses per second. Password hashing functions are deliberately slow, take a cost setting that can be raised over time, and come with a salt, so each guess costs far more and must be made for each account separately."
      - q: "An attacker records your TLS 1.3 traffic today and steals the server's private key next year. Can they read the recording?"
        a: "No. TLS 1.3 uses a fresh key exchange for every connection and throws the keys away: the server's private key only signs the handshake. That's forward secrecy.       - q: "On café Wi-Fi, what does HTTPS hide from someone watching the network, and what can they still see?"
        a: "Hidden: the pages, the full addresses after the site's name, cookies, passwords and everything you send. Still visible: the IP addresses, when you connected, how much data went each way, and usually the site's name, from DNS and the TLS greeting, unless both are encrypted too."
      - q: "A laptop with BitLocker is found running and unlocked during an investigation. Why shouldn't you just switch it off and take the disk?"
        a: "While it runs, the disk is readable and the keys are in memory. Once it's off, the disk is ciphertext, and you need the recovery password. SWGDE notes a live acquisition may be the only chance to get the data, and that capturing memory first may recover the keys. With a TPM, the disk is also tied to that computer.       - q: "The verification hash of a disk image doesn't match the acquisition hash. What does that mean, and what do you do?"
        a: "The image is not an exact copy of what was read, so it can't be used as one. Record it, check the logs for the cause (read errors, a failing drive, a bad connection) and acquire again. A matching pair of hashes is what lets you say later that every bit is the same."

        a: "Not necessarily. The signature proves who signed it and that nothing changed since. It says nothing about whether the app is harmless: a publisher can sign a bad update, and a stolen signing key makes valid signatures too."

## The short version

Think of sending something valuable by post.

- A **strongbox with two identical keys**, one for you and one for the person you write to: **symmetric
  encryption**. It's quick, but first you have to get the second key to them without anyone copying it.
- An **open padlock that anyone can have**, whose only key stays with its owner: **asymmetric (public-key)
  encryption**. Anyone can snap it shut on a box for the owner; only the owner can open it.
- A **fingerprint of the letter**: a **hash**. The same letter always leaves the same fingerprint, a changed letter
  leaves a different one, and nobody can rebuild the letter from the fingerprint.
- A **wax seal pressed with your own ring**: a **digital signature**. Anyone can check it's your seal and that it's
  unbroken; only your ring makes it.
- A **passport** stamped by an authority everyone trusts: a **certificate**. It says whose padlock is whose.
- A **courier** who checks the passport, uses the padlocks to set up a fresh strongbox, then carries everything in
  it: **TLS**, the protection behind HTTPS.
- And **rewriting the letter in another alphabet**, like Morse code, is **encoding** (Base64, hex). It looks
  scrambled, but anyone with the chart can read it: there's no key at all.

<mark>Encryption hides data from anyone without the key and can be undone with it. Hashing turns data into a short
fingerprint that can't be undone. Signing proves who made something and that nobody changed it. Encoding only changes
the alphabet. Knowing which one was used, on what, and who holds the keys tells you what's protected and what
isn't.</mark> Security work meets all of it every day: in file hashes, password databases, certificates, HTTPS
traffic and encrypted disks.

By the end of this page you'll be able to:

- tell encoding, encryption and hashing apart, and recognize each by how it looks;
- explain symmetric and asymmetric encryption, why real systems use both together, and which algorithms and key
  sizes are in use today;
- name MD5, SHA-1 and SHA-256 by their length, say what each is still good for, and check a file's hash;
- explain why passwords are stored as slow, salted hashes, and recognize the common password-hash formats;
- say what a digital signature and a certificate prove, and how a browser checks a chain of certificates;
- walk through a TLS 1.3 connection and say what it hides and what it leaves visible;
- explain what disk encryption protects and what it doesn't, and why that matters when a computer is seized;
- explain how investigators use hashes to show that a copy of evidence is exact.

## Words you'll need

- **Plaintext and ciphertext:** the readable data, and the scrambled result of encrypting it.
- **Key:** the secret value that turns plaintext into ciphertext and back. The method is public; only the key is
  secret.
- **Algorithm (or cipher):** the published method, such as AES. Its security rests on the key, not on hiding how it
  works.
- **Key length:** a key's size in bits. A 128-bit key has 2¹²⁸ possible values, a number with 39 digits.
- **Hexadecimal (hex):** writing bytes with the digits 0–9 and the letters a–f. Each hex character stands for 4
  bits, so one byte takes two characters.
- **Nonce:** a "number used once", a fresh value mixed into each encryption so the same message never encrypts the
  same way twice.

## Encoding, encryption and hashing

Three things that look alike on screen and do completely different jobs:

| | Encoding | Encryption | Hashing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Examples | Base64, hex, URL encoding | AES, ChaCha20 | SHA-256, MD5 |
| Needs a key? | No | Yes | No |
| Can be undone? | By anyone | Only with the key | No |
| Output | Longer than the input | About as long as the input, plus a little | Always the same length |
| Used for | Carrying data through systems built for text | Keeping data secret | Checking that data is unchanged, identifying files |

<!-- pic: three -->

**Base64** (RFC 4648) writes any bytes with 64 safe characters: A–Z, a–z, 0–9, `+` and `/`, with `=` as padding at
the end. Every 3 bytes become 4 characters, so the result is about a third longer than the original. It's used
wherever binary data has to travel through something built for text. **Hex** (Base16 in the same RFC) writes each
byte as two characters and is how hashes are usually shown.

> [!WARNING]
> Encoding is not protection. A password "hidden" as Base64 in a configuration file or a script is as readable as
> plain text to anyone who decodes it. If someone calls Base64 "encrypted", that's a finding.

Attackers use encoding to hide in plain sight. PowerShell's `-EncodedCommand` option runs a command written in
Base64 (of the command's UTF-16LE text), so a long run of letters after `-EncodedCommand`, or a shortened form of it, in a
process list or a log is a command to decode, not noise. Microsoft documents how to decode them from
running processes, and tools like CyberChef do it in the browser.

## Symmetric encryption: one shared key

In **symmetric encryption**, the same key locks and unlocks. It's fast enough to encrypt whole disks and every packet
of a video call, so it does almost all of the actual encrypting you'll meet.

### AES and ChaCha20

**AES** (Advanced Encryption Standard) is the one to know. NIST published it as FIPS 197 in November 2001, after an
open competition, and updated the document in May 2023 without changing the algorithm. AES works on blocks of 128
bits (16 bytes), with keys of 128, 192 or 256 bits: that's what AES-128, AES-192 and AES-256 mean. All three are
approved.

**ChaCha20-Poly1305** (RFC 8439) is the main alternative. Its RFC puts it at about three times as fast as AES on
devices without special AES hardware, and it's one of the three cipher suites TLS 1.3 offers.

Retired ciphers you'll still find in old systems and old reports: **DES** and **Triple DES (3DES)**. NIST allowed
3DES only until the end of 2023.

### Modes: how a block cipher handles more than one block

AES on its own encrypts exactly 16 bytes. A **mode of operation** decides how to handle longer data, and it matters
as much as the cipher:

- **ECB** encrypts every block separately, so identical blocks of plaintext give identical blocks of ciphertext, and
  patterns show through. NIST's 2024 draft of SP 800-131A proposes to stop allowing ECB for keeping data secret.
- **GCM** (NIST SP 800-38D, 2007) is **authenticated encryption**: it encrypts and also adds a short **tag**, so
  any change to the ciphertext is detected and decryption fails instead of returning tampered data. It needs a new
  nonce for every message under the same key. ChaCha20-Poly1305 works the same way, and its RFC calls the unique
  nonce its most important security consideration: reuse one, and the protection breaks.
- **XTS** (NIST SP 800-38E, 2010) is made for disks. It mixes each block's position on the disk into the
  encryption, so the same data in two places looks different. It keeps data secret but, as NIST notes, doesn't
  authenticate it: there's no tag to detect changes.

> [!TIP]
> In code reviews and configurations, look for the mode, not just "AES". `AES-256-GCM` is good; `AES-128-ECB`
> isn't, even though both say AES.

### The catch: sharing the key

Both sides need the same key, and anyone who gets a copy can read everything. Sending the key over the network
you're trying to protect defeats the point. Until the 1970s, the only answer was to agree on keys in advance, by
courier or in person.

## Asymmetric encryption: a pair of keys

In 1976, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published "New Directions in Cryptography", which introduced
**public-key cryptography** and digital signatures; it earned them the 2015 Turing Award. In 1977, Ron Rivest, Adi
Shamir and Leonard Adleman at MIT built **RSA**, the system that made the idea practical, and won the Turing Award
in 2002.

Everyone who uses it has a **key pair**:

- a **public key**, which can be shared with anyone, even posted online;
- a **private key**, which never leaves its owner.

The two are linked by math, and the private key can't be worked out from the public one. That gives two jobs:

| To… | you use… | so that… |
|---|---|---|
| send Anna a secret | Anna's **public** key | only Anna's private key can open it |
| sign something | your own **private** key | anyone can check it with your public key |
| check Anna's signature | Anna's **public** key | you know only her private key could have made it |

### Key exchange: agreeing on a secret in public

**Diffie-Hellman key exchange** lets two sides that have never met agree on a shared secret over an open line. Each
picks a private number and sends the other a value made from it; each then combines the other's value with its own
private number. Both end up with the same secret, and someone who saw everything they sent can't compute it. Done
with elliptic curves, it's called **ECDHE**: the final E stands for ephemeral, a new key pair for every connection,
for example on the curve X25519.

### Hybrid encryption: how real systems do it

Public-key math is slow and can only handle small amounts of data, so nobody encrypts a whole file or web page with
RSA directly. Real systems combine the two kinds:

1. Public-key cryptography agrees on, or carries, a fresh random symmetric key.
2. AES or ChaCha20 encrypts the actual data with that key.

TLS works this way, and so does encrypted email.

### Key sizes: what "strong enough" means

Symmetric and public-key keys can't be compared by length: a 2048-bit RSA key is about as hard to break as a 112-bit
symmetric key. NIST's comparison, from SP 800-57:

| Security strength | Symmetric | RSA | Elliptic curve |
|---|---|---|---|
| 112 bits | 3DES (retired) | 2048 | 224–255 |
| 128 bits | AES-128 | 3072 | 256–383, such as P-256 |
| 192 bits | AES-192 | 7680 | 384–511 |
| 256 bits | AES-256 | 15360 | 512 and more |

Elliptic-curve keys are much shorter for the same strength. NIST accepts 112
bits, and so RSA-2048, for protecting new data only through 2030; from 2031, the minimum is 128.

### Quantum computers and the move to new algorithms

A large enough quantum computer could solve the math that RSA and elliptic curves rely on. None exists yet, but
anything recorded today could be decrypted once one does: the **"harvest now, decrypt later"** threat. Symmetric
ciphers and hashes are far less affected: NIST doesn't require moving off AES or the SHA-2 hashes.

On 13 August 2024, NIST approved the first post-quantum standards: FIPS 203 (**ML-KEM**, for agreeing on keys), and
FIPS 204 (**ML-DSA**) and FIPS 205 (**SLH-DSA**), both for signatures. Its transition plan, NIST IR 8547 (October
2026), deprecates RSA and elliptic-curve algorithms after 2030 and disallows them after 2035.

The web isn't waiting. Browsers already combine old and new in a **hybrid** key exchange, **X25519MLKEM768**
(X25519 and ML-KEM together, so the connection stays safe as long as either one holds). Chrome has used it by
default since version 131, released in November 2024, and the IETF standardized it as RFC 10024 in August 2026. In April
2026, Cloudflare reported that over 65% of human traffic to its network was protected this way. Post-quantum
signatures and certificates are further behind.

## Hashes: fingerprints for data

A **cryptographic hash function** turns any amount of data, a word or a whole 2 TB disk, into a short value of
fixed length: the **hash**, also called a digest. Good ones have four properties:

1. **Same input, same hash:** every time, on every computer.
2. **Any change shows:** change a single bit of the input and the hash changes completely.
3. **One-way:** you can't work back from the hash to the input.
4. **Collision-resistant:** it's practically impossible to find two different inputs with the same hash.

### The ones you'll meet

| Hash | Standard | Bits | Hex characters | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MD5 | RFC 1321, 1992 | 128 | 32 | Collisions since 2004. Not for signatures; still fine for spotting accidental changes |
| SHA-1 | FIPS 180-1, 1995 | 160 | 40 | Collisions shown in 2017. NIST wants it gone by the end of 2030 |
| SHA-256 | FIPS 180-4 (SHA-2) | 256 | 64 | Current. The default in Windows' Get-FileHash |
| SHA-512 | FIPS 180-4 (SHA-2) | 512 | 128 | Same family, longer |
| SHA3-256 | FIPS 202 (SHA-3) | 256 | 64 | A different design, approved as an alternative to SHA-2 |

Counting characters is the quickest way to guess what you're looking at: 32 is MD5, 40 is SHA-1, 64 is SHA-256.
The length gives the size, not the algorithm, though: a Windows NT password hash is also 32 hex characters, and
SHA3-256 is also 64.

### What a collision means

On 23 February 2017, Google and CWI Amsterdam published **SHAttered**: two different PDF files with the same SHA-1
hash. Finding them took 9,223,372,036,854,775,808 SHA-1 computations, about 6,500 years of work for one CPU plus 110
years for one GPU, yet over 100,000 times fewer than trying at random.

Why it matters: a signature is made over the hash. If two documents share a hash, a signature on the harmless one
also fits the harmful one. So MD5 and SHA-1 must not be used where someone could prepare such a pair in advance:
signatures, certificates, anything an attacker can influence. RFC 6151 says exactly that for MD5.

What they're still fine for: noticing accidental changes, such as a corrupted download or a failing disk. The known
attacks make two new files that match each other; making a file match a hash someone already recorded is still far
out of reach: RFC 6151 puts the best such attack on MD5 at about 2¹²³ steps.

### Where hashes show up

- **Checking downloads:** publishers list a SHA-256 next to the file; you compute it yourself and compare.
- **Naming files:** a file's hash stays the same whatever the file is called, so threat reports and malware
  databases identify samples by their SHA-256.
- **Setting known files aside:** NIST's National Software Reference Library (NSRL) publishes the hashes of known
  software files, free, four times a year. Investigators load it to filter out the operating system and program
  files they don't need to look at. "Known" doesn't mean "good": it includes hacking tools too.
- **Proving evidence is unchanged**, next.
- **Passwords and signatures**, further down this page.

A hash with a secret key mixed in is an **HMAC** (FIPS 198-1): only someone who holds the key can make or check it.
APIs use HMACs to prove that a request came from someone with the key and wasn't changed on the way.

### Hashes as evidence seals

When investigators copy a disk, they hash it, following guidance such as SWGDE's:

1. **Acquisition hash:** computed while the image is being made, from the data as it's read.
2. **Verification hash:** computed again from the finished image, before anyone analyzes it. If the two match, the
   image is an exact copy of what was read.
3. **Rehash at the end:** shows the image didn't change during the examination, and lets anyone check later that
   it's still a true copy of the original.

SWGDE asks for a NIST-approved hash algorithm. You'll often see two hashes in an imaging log, an older one such as
MD5 next to SHA-256: the first to match older records and tools, the second for strength.

> [!NOTE]
> A matching hash proves the copy is identical. It doesn't prove anything about the contents: who put the files
> there, or whether they tell the truth.

## Storing passwords: slow hashes with salt

A website shouldn't be able to tell you your password, only check it. So passwords are **hashed, not encrypted**:
when you sign in, the site hashes what you typed and compares the result with the stored hash.

A plain SHA-256 is the wrong tool, though. It's built to be fast, so someone who steals the hashes can test enormous
numbers of guesses per second on their own computers: **offline cracking**, one of the
[password attacks](/en/roadmap/topics/common-attacks/#password-attacks). Two things fix that:

- **Salt:** a random value, different for every account, mixed in before hashing and stored next to the hash. Two
  people with the same password get different hashes, and tables of precomputed hashes (rainbow tables) become
  useless. NIST SP 800-63B-4 asks for salts of at least 32 bits.
- **Slowness:** a password hashing function is deliberately expensive, with a **cost** setting that can be raised as
  computers get faster. NIST says the cost should be as high as practical; OWASP suggests one hash should still take
  less than a second.

OWASP's recommendations, best first:

| Algorithm | OWASP's minimum settings | When |
|---|---|---|
| Argon2id | 19 MiB of memory, 2 passes, 1 thread | The first choice |
| scrypt | N=2¹⁷ (128 MiB), r=8, p=1 | When Argon2id isn't available |
| bcrypt | Cost 10 | Older systems only. Most versions read only the first 72 bytes of the password |
| PBKDF2 | 600,000 rounds of HMAC-SHA-256 | When FIPS-140 compliance is required |

NIST also suggests a **pepper**: one more keyed step with a secret key that's stored apart from the database,
ideally in hardware, so a stolen database alone isn't enough to start guessing.

<!-- pic: shapes -->

On Linux, `/etc/shadow` holds hashes in exactly these formats: the method, its settings, the salt and the hash in
one string, separated by `$`. Current Debian uses yescrypt (see [Linux basics](/en/roadmap/topics/linux-basics/#accounts-are-lines-in-a-text-file)).
Windows is the odd one out. The **NT hash** stored in the SAM (see [Windows basics](/en/roadmap/topics/windows-basics/#accounts-groups-and-sids))
is MD4 of the password's UTF-16 text, with no salt and no cost setting. The same password gives the same NT hash on
every Windows computer, and it's fast to compute, which is why stolen NT hashes are cracked so quickly.

> [!NOTE]
> Hashing protects stored passwords, not weak ones. A slow, salted hash of `Winter2026!` still falls to a good list
> of guesses. NIST asks for at least 15 characters for a password that's used on its own, without MFA.

## Digital signatures: who made it, and that it's unchanged

A **digital signature** proves two things at once: the data hasn't changed since it was signed (**integrity**), and
it was signed by whoever holds a particular private key (**authenticity**). Because only the signer has that key,
they can't easily deny having signed it (**non-repudiation**), as long as the key was kept safe.

<!-- pic: signature -->

NIST's Digital Signature Standard, FIPS 186-5 (3 February 2023), approves three algorithms: **RSA**, **ECDSA** and
**EdDSA** (such as Ed25519), the last one new in this version. The older DSA is kept only to check existing
signatures. ML-DSA and SLH-DSA are the post-quantum signatures that come next.

You'll meet signatures on:

- **software and updates:** operating systems check signatures on apps, drivers and updates, and Linux package
  managers check one on every package;
- **certificates**, which are signed by the authority that issued them;
- **TLS**, where the server signs the handshake to prove it holds its certificate's private key;
- **documents and email**, such as signed PDFs and signed email.

An HMAC also proves a message wasn't changed, but anyone who holds its key can make one, so it can't say *which* of
them did. A signature can only be made with the private key, and anyone can check it.

> [!WARNING]
> A valid signature says who signed and that nothing changed since. It doesn't say the signed thing is safe: a
> publisher can sign a bad update, and an attacker who steals a signing key makes valid signatures too.

## Certificates and the chain of trust

A public key is just a long number. Before you trust a signature made with it, or encrypt something to it, you need
to know whose it is. A **certificate** answers that: a small signed document in the X.509 format (RFC 5280) that
says "this public key belongs to this name", signed by a **certificate authority (CA)**.

<!-- pic: cert -->

The fields worth knowing:

- **Subject** and **Subject Alternative Name:** who the certificate is for. The alternative names list every
  domain name it covers.
- **Issuer:** the CA that signed it.
- **Validity:** the two dates it's valid between. Public website certificates now last at most 200 days (see
  [How the web works](/en/roadmap/topics/web-basics/#what-a-certificate-proves)).
- **Public key:** the key being vouched for, and its algorithm.
- **Serial number:** unique among one issuer's certificates; it identifies a certificate when it's revoked
  (cancelled before its end date).
- **Signature:** the issuer's signature over everything above.

### The chain

A browser doesn't know every CA. It trusts a set of **root CAs** whose certificates come built into the operating
system or the browser, in the **trust store**. Roots usually sign **intermediate CAs**, and those sign websites'
certificates.

<!-- pic: chain -->

To accept a site's certificate, the browser checks the whole path (RFC 5280 calls it path validation): each
signature with the key from the certificate above it, the dates, that the site's name is among the alternative
names, and whether a certificate has been revoked. One failed check, and you get a full-page warning instead of the
site.

> [!NOTE]
> Companies also run their own private CAs for internal sites, Wi-Fi and devices. Their certificates are trusted
> only on computers where the company added its root to the trust store, which is also how some companies inspect
> their employees' HTTPS traffic.

## TLS: everything working together

[HTTPS](/en/roadmap/topics/web-basics/#https-the-private-line) is HTTP inside TLS, and TLS uses every piece on this
page. Here's a TLS 1.3 connection (RFC 9846), from the first message to the first encrypted request:

<!-- pic: tls -->

A TLS 1.3 **cipher suite** names only the symmetric part. `TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256` means AES-128 in GCM mode, with
SHA-256 used to derive the keys. There are only five. Every TLS 1.3 implementation must support
`TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256` and should support `TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384` and `TLS_CHACHA20_POLY1305_SHA256`. The key
exchange is negotiated separately, as a **group**: P-256 is required, X25519 recommended, and X25519MLKEM768 is the
post-quantum hybrid that Chrome, Edge and Firefox now offer.

Two design choices matter for anyone investigating traffic:

- **Forward secrecy.** TLS 1.3 dropped the old key exchanges that reused the server's long-term key: every
  connection gets fresh keys that are thrown away afterwards, and the server's private key only signs. So a
  recording of the traffic can't be decrypted later, even with the server's private key. You'd need the keys of
  that very session, captured at one end; how comes with network traffic analysis, further down the path.
- **Less in the clear.** Everything after the ServerHello is encrypted, so a capture shows much less than with TLS
  1.2: not even the server's certificate.

## What's protected and what isn't

Encryption protects data in one place, against particular people. The question is always: where is the data now,
and who holds the key?

| Protection | Protects against | Doesn't protect against |
|---|---|---|
| **HTTPS (TLS)**, data in transit | Anyone on the network path reading or changing the traffic | The two ends: the server, its logs and database, malware or a person at the device. It also leaves visible who talks to whom, when, and how much |
| **Disk encryption**, data at rest | Someone who takes a switched-off device or pulls out its disk | Anyone, or any malware, using the computer while it's on and unlocked: files are decrypted as they're read |
| **End-to-end encrypted messages** | The service that carries them | Someone with the unlocked phone, or a copy kept somewhere without that encryption |
| **Password hashing** | A stolen database being read as plain passwords | Weak passwords, which still fall to guessing, and passwords caught as they're typed or phished |
| **A hash listed next to a file** | Not noticing an accidental change | Someone who changes both the file and the hash next to it |
| **Base64 and other encodings** | Nothing | Anyone |

What a network observer still sees with HTTPS, and how much of it encrypted DNS and Encrypted Client Hello can hide,
is covered in [How the web works](/en/roadmap/topics/web-basics/#one-visit-start-to-finish).

### Disk encryption on the three systems

<!-- pic: disk -->

On many Windows devices, BitLocker turns on by itself as **Device Encryption** when someone signs in with a
Microsoft, work or school account, and the recovery key is saved to that account. On Macs with Apple silicon or a T2
chip, the internal disk is encrypted even with FileVault off, but then its key is protected only by the hardware:
turning FileVault on is what ties it to the user's password.

### What it means for an investigation

Disk encryption changes the first minutes of a case:

- A **running, unlocked** computer has its disk readable and its keys in memory. SWGDE notes that a live acquisition
  may be the only opportunity to get data from an encrypted system, and that capturing memory first may recover the
  keys. Pulling the plug can lock everything.
- A **switched-off** encrypted computer needs its key: a recovery password from the company's directory or the
  owner's account, or a passphrase from the owner. Recovery keys may be kept on the computer, on removable media,
  with a cloud provider or in an enterprise management system. Without one, the data may be lost for good.
- With BitLocker and a TPM, the disk is tied to that computer, so SWGDE advises against simply removing it, and
  changing firmware settings such as Secure Boot can make BitLocker ask for the recovery key.

How to handle all of this properly comes with handling encrypted evidence, further down the path.

## How it shows up on the job

Here's a made-up imaging log, the kind a forensic tool writes while copying a laptop's drive:

```
Made-up imaging log · case 2026-117 · laptop LT-0423 · times in UTC

14:02  Source       NVMe SSD, 1,024,209,543,168 bytes, serial EX-0001
14:02  Encryption   BitLocker on volume C: (XTS-AES 128); recovery password
                    from the company directory
14:02  Write block  hardware write blocker, read-only confirmed
16:47  Acquired     LT-0423.E01, 1,024,209,543,168 bytes, 0 read errors
16:47  MD5          64a89d0f313fa6456d9f4c09dc0ca3af
16:47  SHA-256      7f8ba77c1ab8f2211754a5426e6030ef147a09f6130948c87581d7de1bbd6390
16:48  Verify       reading the image back
17:31  MD5          64a89d0f313fa6456d9f4c09dc0ca3af
17:31  SHA-256      7f8ba77c1ab8f2211754a5426e6030ef147a09f6130948c87581d7de1bbd6390
17:31  Result       verified: both hashes match
```

Read it piece by piece:

1. **Source.** The exact size and serial number tie the image to this one drive.
2. **Encryption.** Volume C: uses BitLocker with XTS-AES 128, Windows' default. The image holds those encrypted
   bytes exactly as they are on the disk; to read the files, the analysis tool will need the 48-digit recovery
   password. The company had it in its directory, where its laptops back up their recovery keys. Without it, this
   image would be a terabyte of ciphertext.
3. **Write block.** The write blocker lets the computer read the drive but not change it, so the original stays as
   it was.
4. **16:47, the acquisition hashes.** Computed while reading: an MD5 (32 hex characters) for older records and
   tools, and a SHA-256 (64) as the strong one.
5. **17:31, the verification hashes.** The tool read the finished image back and hashed it again. Both match, so the
   image is a bit-for-bit copy of what was read. If a single character differed, the copy couldn't be trusted: the
   examiner would record it, look for the cause (read errors, a failing drive) and acquire again.
6. **What comes next.** The analysis runs on a working copy, and hashing the image again at the end shows it never
   changed.

- **Digital forensics and incident response:** you hash every image and file you collect, set known files aside
  with hash sets such as the NSRL, spot known malware by its hash, and plan for encrypted disks before you touch a
  running computer.
- **Security operations (SOC) and threat intelligence:** you look files up by SHA-256, decode Base64 in command
  lines, and read TLS details and certificates to judge a connection.
- **Penetration testing and red teaming:** with permission, you crack the password hashes you obtained to show how
  weak the passwords are, and report outdated TLS, ECB, MD5 or SHA-1 wherever they protect something.
- **Application security and development:** you store passwords with Argon2id or bcrypt, use authenticated
  encryption such as AES-GCM from a well-tested library, and keep keys out of the code.
- **PKI, identity and infrastructure:** you issue and renew certificates, protect private keys, and plan the move to
  post-quantum algorithms.
- **GRC, audit and privacy:** you check that data is encrypted at rest and in transit, that someone manages the
  keys, and that claims of "encryption" hold up.
- **Malware analysis:** you meet ransomware that encrypts files, settings hidden with Base64, and samples known by
  their hashes.

## Try it yourself

About fifteen minutes. Nothing here changes your system, and the one file you create, you delete at the end.

### Encoding is not encryption

Turn a sentence into Base64 and back:

<!-- for: windows -->

```powershell
$b = [Convert]::ToBase64String([Text.Encoding]::UTF8.GetBytes("Pay 100 to Alex"))
$b
[Text.Encoding]::UTF8.GetString([Convert]::FromBase64String($b))
```

<!-- for: macos, linux -->

```sh
printf 'Pay 100 to Alex' | base64
printf 'UGF5IDEwMCB0byBBbGV4' | base64 --decode; echo
```

<!-- /for -->

You'll see `UGF5IDEwMCB0byBBbGV4`, then the sentence again. No key was involved anywhere. What would someone need to
read a password stored this way?

### Three hashes, three lengths

Make a small file, hash it three ways, rename it and hash it again:

<!-- for: windows -->

```powershell
Set-Content note.txt "Pay 100 to Alex"
Get-FileHash note.txt -Algorithm MD5
Get-FileHash note.txt -Algorithm SHA1
Get-FileHash note.txt
Rename-Item note.txt renamed.txt
Get-FileHash renamed.txt
Remove-Item renamed.txt
```

`Get-FileHash` uses SHA-256 unless you ask for another algorithm.

<!-- for: macos -->

```sh
echo "Pay 100 to Alex" > note.txt
md5 note.txt
shasum -a 1 note.txt
shasum -a 256 note.txt
mv note.txt renamed.txt
shasum -a 256 renamed.txt
rm renamed.txt
```

<!-- for: linux -->

```sh
echo "Pay 100 to Alex" > note.txt
md5sum note.txt
sha1sum note.txt
sha256sum note.txt
mv note.txt renamed.txt
sha256sum renamed.txt
rm renamed.txt
```

<!-- /for -->

On Linux, the first three hashes look like this (macOS prints the MD5 as `MD5 (note.txt) = …`, and Windows gets
different values, because PowerShell ends the line differently):

```
d08ad59cce6e7f4784a80da330fbfff0  note.txt
ee009c388a1ece9751379b20687e8c9a7277a2fe  note.txt
e0b2c4d048c516fcd09ea13cfb7196b588459725dd5a369d8c14cc3c950f7f6a  note.txt
```

Count the characters: 32, 40 and 64. Did renaming the file change its SHA-256? Why not, and why does that make
hashes useful for recognizing a file? To check a real download against the hash its publisher lists, follow the
step-by-step guide.

### Is your disk encrypted?

<!-- for: windows -->

In **Settings**, open **Privacy & security > Device encryption**, if your device shows it. On Windows Pro,


```sh
fdesetup status
```

It answers `FileVault is On.` or `FileVault is Off.`


### See what protects your connection

Open any HTTPS site in your browser.

- **Chrome:** open DevTools (F12), then the **Privacy and security** panel (under **More tools**, if it isn't
  shown), and select the site in the **Security** section to see its connection and certificate.
- **Firefox:** right-click the page, choose **View Page Info**, then **Security**. **Technical Details** show the
  cipher suite and the TLS version.

Look for TLS 1.3, a cipher such as AES-128-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305 and, in Chrome, X25519MLKEM768 as the key
exchange if the site supports post-quantum. Then open the certificate: who issued it, when does it expire, and how
many certificates are in its chain?
