## The short version

Think of a small shop.

- A caller says they're from the bank and asks for the code to the safe: **phishing**, a form of social engineering
  (tricking a person rather than breaking a lock).
- A "free" gadget left at the counter quietly copies every card at the till: **malware**. Someone padlocks the
  stockroom and leaves a note with a price for the key: **ransomware**.
- Someone tries code after code on the back-door keypad: **password guessing**. Someone tries codes that worked at
  other shops, because owners reuse them: **credential stuffing**.
- A hundred fake customers fill the shop so real ones can't get in: **denial of service**.
- An order form the clerk follows word for word, even when someone writes "and open the till": **SQL injection**.
  A note pinned to the shop's own noticeboard that fools the next customers: **cross-site scripting**.

<mark>Most attacks don't need brilliant hacking. They trick a person, reuse a stolen password, flood a service or
slip instructions into data that a program trusts. Each one leaves traces, and each one has defenses that work:
knowing how an attack works tells you what to look for and what stops it.</mark> Every security job, from the help
desk to incident response, meets these attacks every week.

By the end of this page you'll be able to:

- say what attackers are usually after and name the most common ways they get in;
- take a phishing message apart and explain why phishing-resistant MFA beats codes and push prompts;
- tell viruses, worms, Trojans, infostealers and ransomware apart, and describe how a ransomware attack unfolds;
- tell password guessing, spraying, stuffing and cracking apart, and name the defense for each;
- explain DoS and DDoS, the three kinds of DDoS, and why amplification works;
- explain SQL injection and cross-site scripting as "data treated as code", and name the fix for each;
- read a sign-in and web log and spot credential stuffing and injection probing.

## Words you'll need

- **Social engineering:** getting a person to do something that helps the attacker: reveal a password, open a file,
  approve a payment.
- **Credentials:** whatever proves who you are to a system: a username and password, a code, or the session cookie a
  site gives you after you sign in.
- **Malware:** software made to harm the confidentiality, integrity or availability of a system or its data.
- **Botnet:** many computers or devices taken over and controlled remotely, used together, for example to flood a
  website.
- **Initial access:** the attacker's first foothold, such as one working account or one compromised server.
- **Exploit:** a way, often a program, to take advantage of a vulnerability.

## What attackers are after

Behind almost every attack is one of a few goals: **money** (a ransom, a fraudulent payment, stolen cards), **data**
to sell or to threaten to publish, **access** to use later or to sell, **disruption**, or **spying**. The same
technique serves different goals: a phished password can lead to a fraudulent wire transfer or to months of quiet
espionage.

<!-- pic: glance -->

Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report looked at more than 22,000 confirmed breaches. The most common ways
in were exploiting a software vulnerability (31%), phishing (16%) and stolen credentials (13%).
A person who was tricked or made a mistake played a part in 62% of breaches, and ransomware was present in 48% of them.
Exploiting vulnerabilities and patching them get their own topics further down the path; this page covers the rest.

> [!NOTE]
> A **breach** means data was confirmed to have been exposed. Verizon counts a denial-of-service attack as an
> **incident** but usually not a breach: it stops a service without stealing anything.

## Phishing and social engineering

MITRE ATT&CK, the public catalog of attacker techniques, defines phishing (T1566) as sending messages to gain access
to victims' systems, and calls all forms of it electronically delivered social engineering. **Spearphishing** targets
one person, company or industry; mass phishing goes to anyone. ATT&CK splits it by how the lure arrives:

| Sub-technique | How the lure arrives |
|---|---|
| T1566.001 Spearphishing Attachment | A file in an email |
| T1566.002 Spearphishing Link | A link to a fake page or a download |
| T1566.003 Spearphishing via Service | Social media, chat apps and other services instead of company email |
| T1566.004 Spearphishing Voice | A phone call, often from someone posing as IT support |

Phishing by text message is often called **smishing**, by voice **vishing**. They work well: in Verizon's phishing
simulations, people clicked about 40% more often on lures sent by text message or voice than by email.

A joint guide from CISA, the NSA, the FBI and MS-ISAC sorts phishing by what it wants:

- **Credentials:** a fake sign-in page collects the username, password and even the MFA code.
- **Malware:** an attachment or link that runs malware on the victim's computer.

A third goal is money directly. In **business email compromise (BEC)**, criminals pose as a supplier or a boss and ask
for a payment to a new bank account. In 2025, phishing and spoofing was the crime type most often reported to the FBI
(191,561 complaints), and BEC cost the people and companies who reported it more than $3 billion.

### When MFA isn't enough

Codes and push approvals stop attackers who only have a password, so phishing adapted. In **adversary-in-the-middle
(AiTM)** phishing, the fake page sits between the victim and the real site and passes everything along: the victim
signs in to the real service, MFA included, and the attacker keeps the **session cookie** the site hands back. With
that cookie, they are signed in as the victim without needing the password or the code again. Microsoft described one
AiTM campaign that went after more than 10,000 organizations from September 2021, and stressed that this is not a
vulnerability in MFA: the attacker steals the session after the sign-in. **Phishing-resistant MFA** (passkeys and
security keys built on FIDO) stops it, because it only answers the real site; [Security fundamentals](/en/roadmap/topics/security-fundamentals/)
explains why.

Phone calls do the same job. ATT&CK records the Scattered Spider group calling employees while posing as their IT
department and talking them into installing a remote-control tool.

### ClickFix: when the victim runs the code

A newer trick skips the attachment altogether. A page shows a fake error or a "verify you are human" box and tells the
visitor to open the Windows Run dialog, Windows Terminal or PowerShell (or Terminal on a Mac), paste a command and press
Enter. Microsoft calls this **ClickFix** and reported campaigns aimed at thousands of devices every day in 2025,
arriving through phishing emails, malicious ads and hacked legitimate websites. Because a person starts the command,
automated defenses can miss it. The usual result is an infostealer or a remote-access tool.

> [!WARNING]
> No real CAPTCHA, update or "fix" asks you to paste a command into Run, Terminal or PowerShell. Close the page.

### What stops phishing

"For too long, the prevailing guidance to prevent phishing attacks has been for users to avoid clicking on malicious
emails. We know that this advice is not sufficient," CISA said when it published the joint guide. Its advice combines
people and technology:

- **Training and an easy report button**, so suspicious messages reach the security team quickly.
- **Email authentication:** DMARC, with SPF and DKIM, lets receiving servers check that mail claiming to come from a
  domain really does, and reject what doesn't.
- **Phishing-resistant MFA** (FIDO or PKI-based); where push approvals stay, turn on number matching.
- **Blocking** known bad domains and risky file types, blocking Office macros by default and allowing only approved
  applications.
- **No admin rights** for everyday accounts, so a click can't install anything system-wide.

## Malware and ransomware

NIST defines **malware** as software or firmware meant to do something unauthorized that harms the confidentiality,
integrity or availability of a system. The names describe how it spreads or what it does:

| Type | What it does |
|---|---|
| Virus | Copies itself into other programs or files and runs when they do |
| Worm | Copies itself across a network on its own, without a host program or anyone's help |
| Trojan horse | Looks useful but hides malicious code that runs when you start it |
| Spyware | Installed secretly to gather information about people or organizations |
| Infostealer | Steals passwords, card and bank details and crypto wallets and sends them to the attacker |
| Ransomware | Encrypts files and demands payment to unlock them |
| Bot | Lets an attacker control the device remotely as part of a botnet |

Malware arrives through the routes above: phishing attachments and links, malicious ads, fake download pages, hacked
websites, and, for worms, network services that haven't been updated.

Two real cases show the range:

- **WannaCry (May 2017)** was ransomware that spread like a worm through a flaw in Windows file sharing (SMBv1).
  Microsoft had released the fix, MS17-010, on March 14, 2017, two months before the outbreak on May 12. Computers
  with the fix installed couldn't be infected that way.
- **Lumma Stealer** was an infostealer that "steals passwords, credit cards, bank accounts, and cryptocurrency
  wallets," in Microsoft's words. Between March 16 and May 16, 2025, Microsoft counted more than 394,000 infected
  Windows computers, reached through spear-phishing and malicious ads. After a court action that Microsoft filed on May
  13, 2025, about 2,300 domains behind Lumma were taken down or blocked.

### Ransomware

CISA describes **ransomware** as malware designed to encrypt files on a device. Many gangs now also copy the data out
first and threaten to publish it: CISA calls using both **double extortion**, and some groups skip the encryption
altogether. In Verizon's 2026 report, ransomware was present in 48% of breaches, 69% of victims didn't pay, and the
median ransom paid was $139,875. Akira, Qilin and INC (also called Lynx or Sinobi) were the variants most reported to
the FBI in 2025, a year in which it identified 63 new ones.

<!-- pic: ransom -->

CISA's #StopRansomware Guide groups the ways in into six: internet-facing vulnerabilities and misconfigurations,
compromised credentials, phishing, earlier malware infections, advanced social engineering, and third parties such as
managed service providers. Its first piece of advice is about recovery: keep **offline, encrypted backups** of critical
data and test restoring them, because many ransomware variants look for reachable backups and delete or encrypt them.

> [!NOTE]
> A copy that's always connected is a copy the attackers can reach. CISA asks for backups kept offline (or copied
> cloud-to-cloud), encrypted, and tested in a real recovery drill.

## Password attacks

ATT&CK groups password attacks under **brute force** (T1110): systematically guessing when the password is unknown,
either against a service that checks it (online) or against stolen password hashes (offline). Four kinds matter:

<!-- pic: passwords -->

Two real cases:

- **Credential stuffing at 23andMe (2023).** The genetics company told the SEC that an attacker reached about 0.1% of
  user accounts, those whose usernames and passwords had been used on other websites that were breached earlier.
  Through a sharing feature, those accounts exposed profile information of many more users.
- **Password spraying at Microsoft (2024).** Microsoft said the Midnight Blizzard group got in with a password spray
  against "a legacy, non-production test tenant account that did not have multifactor authentication (MFA) enabled,"
  keeping attempts low and spread over many IP addresses to avoid detection.

What stops them:

- **MFA** turns a correct password into only half of what's needed. Phishing-resistant MFA also survives fake sign-in
  pages.
- **Rate limiting.** NIST's SP 800-63B-4 requires services to limit consecutive failed attempts on one account to no
  more than 100, and suggests bot checks and growing waits between tries. Windows 11 version 22H2 sets up new computers
  to lock an account for 10 minutes after 10 failed attempts within 10 minutes.
- **Blocking bad passwords.** NIST requires checking new passwords against lists of common, expected and leaked ones.
- **A different password for every site**, kept in a password manager, so a leak elsewhere opens nothing here.

> [!WARNING]
> Lockout cuts both ways. ATT&CK notes that too strict a policy can itself cause a denial of service: an attacker can
> lock out every account by failing on purpose. NIST sets 100 failures as an upper limit and suggests growing delays
> and bot checks, which slow attackers down without locking people out.

## Denial of service

A **denial-of-service (DoS)** attack makes a system unavailable by flooding it with traffic or requests. CISA's guide
explains the difference in scale: a DoS attack typically comes from one computer or a few, a **distributed (DDoS)**
attack from a botnet of many compromised devices at once, which produces far more traffic and is harder to block.

<!-- pic: ddos -->

### Amplification

Some services answer a small question with a big reply. In a **reflection and amplification** attack, the attacker
sends small requests to public servers with the victim's address forged as the sender, and the servers send their big
replies to the victim. CISA measures this as a bandwidth amplification factor: about 28 to 54 times for DNS, 556.9 for
NTP, and 10,000 to 51,000 for exposed memcached servers. Its fix is for networks to drop packets with forged sender
addresses (IETF BCP 38).

### How big it gets

In the third quarter of 2026 alone, Cloudflare blocked 18.2 million network-layer DDoS attacks. Most were short and
small: 96% ended in under 10 minutes and 98% stayed under 1 gigabit per second. The giants are rarer but real: 251
attacks topped 1 terabit per second that quarter, down from 805 in the previous one. The drop coincided with law
enforcement dismantling four of the largest botnets (Aisuru, KimWolf, JackSkid and Mossad) earlier in 2026.

What helps, from CISA's guide: a DDoS protection service or content delivery network in front of the site, rate
limiting and filtering, a call to the internet provider during an attack, spare capacity, and a tested response plan.

## Web attacks: SQL injection and cross-site scripting

Both are **injection**: a program treats data from a user as part of its own code. OWASP's Top 10:2025 puts Injection
fifth among web application risks, with more than 30,000 published vulnerabilities (CVEs) for cross-site scripting and
more than 14,000 for SQL injection. In MITRE's 2025 CWE Top 25 of the most dangerous software weaknesses, cross-site
scripting (CWE-79) is first and SQL injection (CWE-89) second.

### SQL injection

Websites keep their data in databases and ask for it in a language called **SQL**. **SQL injection** happens when a
program glues what a user typed straight into an SQL command, so the database can't tell the command from the data.

<!-- pic: sqli -->

Through it, CISA and the FBI warn, attackers can steal, change or delete information in the database, or make it
unavailable. From May 27, 2023, the CL0P ransomware gang used a previously unknown SQL injection flaw (CVE-2023-34362)
in MOVEit Transfer, a file transfer product, to plant a web shell called LEMURLOOT on internet-facing servers and steal
data from them. The campaign hit thousands of organizations.

The fix is to never build commands out of text. Unsafe: the program glues the name into the command, and the
database receives this:

```sql
SELECT * FROM customers WHERE name = 'O'Brien'
```

Safe: with a **parameterized query** (a prepared statement), the command has a placeholder, `?`, and the name travels
separately, so it can never become part of the command:

```sql
SELECT * FROM customers WHERE name = ?
```

CISA and the FBI call input sanitization (removing or escaping dangerous characters) brittle, hard to enforce at scale
and often bypassed, and note that MySQL already offered prepared statements in 2004.

### Cross-site scripting (XSS)

**Cross-site scripting** happens when a site takes data from an untrusted source, usually a web request, and puts it
into a page for other users without encoding it, so their browsers run it as code that seems to come from the site.
OWASP describes three kinds:

- **Stored:** the code is saved on the site, in a comment or a profile, and reaches everyone who views it.
- **Reflected:** the code is in a link, and the site echoes it back in one response to whoever opens the link.
- **DOM-based:** the page's own JavaScript writes the data into the page, sometimes without the server ever seeing it.

<!-- pic: xss -->

What it can lead to, per OWASP: impersonating the user's account, watching what they do, loading outside content and
stealing sensitive data. The defenses, in OWASP's order: frameworks that escape output by default, **output encoding**
for anything they don't cover, sanitizing HTML when users are allowed to write it, and safe ways to write text into a
page (`textContent` rather than `innerHTML`). A **Content Security Policy** is a useful extra layer, but OWASP says it
should not be the main defense. CISA and the FBI point out that effective fixes for cross-site scripting have been
known for over twenty years.

## How it shows up on the job

Here's a made-up excerpt that a SOC analyst might pull from a small online shop's sign-in and web logs:

```
Made-up sign-in and web log · shop.example · 2026-11-12, times in UTC

03:14:01  203.0.113.7     POST /login  anna@mail.example   401 wrong password
03:14:01  198.51.100.23   POST /login  bo@mail.example     401 no such user
03:14:02  203.0.113.91    POST /login  chen@mail.example   401 wrong password
03:14:02  198.51.100.4    POST /login  dana@mail.example   200 signed in
          ...4,800 more in 20 minutes, from 61 addresses: 12 signed in
03:41:10  192.0.2.66      GET  /products?id=12             200
03:41:12  192.0.2.66      GET  /products?id=12%27          500 database error
03:41:15  192.0.2.66      GET  /search?q=%3Cb%3Ehi         200
```

Read it piece by piece:

1. **03:14, many accounts, one try each, many sources.** Different email addresses, each tried once or twice from a
   different address, with "no such user" among the failures: that's **credential stuffing** with a leaked list, not
   guessing at one account. The 12 successes are customers who reused a password from another site.
2. **What to do about it:** block or challenge the source addresses, reset the 12 passwords, check what happened in
   those accounts (orders, saved cards, changed addresses), tell the customers, and offer MFA.
3. **03:41:12, `%27`.** That's an apostrophe, written the way URLs encode it. Adding one to a product number broke a
   database query: someone is checking whether input reaches the database unescaped, the first step in looking for
   **SQL injection**. The error itself is the finding: that query needs a parameter.
4. **03:41:15, `%3Cb%3E`.** That's `<b>`, the HTML tag for bold. Someone is checking whether the search page puts
   what you type into the page as HTML, the first sign of **cross-site scripting**. Whether "hi" came back in bold
   decides how urgent the fix is.

- **Security operations (SOC):** you triage phishing reports, spot credential stuffing and sprays in sign-in logs, and
  decide in minutes whether an account is compromised.
- **Incident response and forensics:** you trace how attackers got in, often to a phishing email or a reused password,
  rebuild a ransomware timeline and check what data left.
- **Penetration testing, red teaming and bug bounty:** with permission, you test for SQL injection and cross-site
  scripting, run phishing simulations and try password sprays against the rules you agreed with the client.
- **Detection engineering and threat hunting:** you write rules for spray patterns, ClickFix commands, new web shells
  and floods of failed sign-ins.
- **Secure development and application security:** you use parameterized queries and auto-escaping templates by
  default and review code for the places where they're missing.
- **Security awareness and GRC:** you run training that people can use, keep a working report button, and measure
  which controls actually stop these attacks.
- **Network and infrastructure:** you put DDoS protection in front of public services and make sure your own servers
  can't be used as amplifiers.

## Try it yourself

About fifteen minutes, nothing to install, and nothing on your computer changes.

### Read links like a defender

Before you look at the right-hand column, decide for each link which site it really goes to:

<!-- pic: links -->

The trick in the third and fifth lines is the one the phishing email above used. On a computer, hover over a link to
see where it goes before you click; on a phone, press and hold it. [How the web works](/en/roadmap/topics/web-basics/#reading-a-url-safely)
explains how URLs are built.

### Has your email address been in a breach?

Open [Have I Been Pwned](https://haveibeenpwned.com/), a free service that, as of November 2026, lists more than 17
billion email addresses from over 1,000 breaches. Enter an address you use.

- If it reports breaches, look at which data leaked. If a password was among it and you used that password anywhere
  else, change it there: that's exactly what credential stuffing tries.
- If it says "Good news — no pwnage found!", that only means the address isn't in the breaches it has loaded.

Either way, turn on MFA for your main email account. Which of your accounts would be at risk if one password leaked?

### How many guesses does your computer allow?

On Windows, open PowerShell (type `powershell` in the Start menu) and run:

```powershell
net accounts
```

This only reads the settings. Look at **Lockout threshold**: the number of failed sign-ins before the account locks,
or **Never** if it never does. **Lockout duration** and **Lockout observation window** are the other two minutes-based
settings. New computers set up with Windows 11 version 22H2 or later start with 10, 10 and 10. What would a password
sprayer have to do to stay under your limit? On macOS and Linux, lockout rules live elsewhere and depend on how the
system was set up.
